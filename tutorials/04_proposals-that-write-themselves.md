# Tutorial 04: Proposals That Write Themselves
**Series:** Design Studio OS | Design Thread Studio
**Runtime:** ~5 minutes
**Format:** Talking head + screen share demo

---

## PRE-PRODUCTION NOTES

- **Tone:** Confident, a little conspiratorial — "let me show you a secret." This is the tutorial that will convert skeptics.
- **Screen share:** A real (or realistic mock) proposal document before and after AI assistance. Show the before as a half-filled blank template. Show the after as polished first draft.
- **B-roll:** Staring at a blank document, checking the clock — the emotional setup of the problem.
- **Key stat to mention:** Many boutique designers spend 3–6 hours per proposal on average.

---

## SCRIPT

**[OPEN ON: B-roll — blank document open, cursor blinking, clock on the wall. Cut to camera.]**

The proposal is where a lot of designers quietly lose hours every week.

Not because you don't know what you want to say. You do. You've done the discovery call. You know the space. You know the client. The ideas are there.

But sitting down to put all of that into a document that is clear, professional, persuasive, and sounds like you — while also running the actual business — is exhausting. And it happens on every single project.

**[Camera, direct.]**

The average boutique designer I've spoken with spends three to six hours writing a proposal. I want to get that down to under one. Here's how.

**[TRANSITION TO SCREEN SHARE: Claude.ai]**

The key is understanding what AI is actually good at in proposal writing. It's good at structure. It's good at formal language. It's good at taking your rough ideas and making them sound polished. And it's very good at the boilerplate — the sections that are similar project to project but that you still have to write fresh every time.

Here's my proposal prompt. I call it the First Draft Engine.

**[SHOW ON SCREEN. Read deliberately.]**

> *"You are helping a boutique interior designer write a client proposal. Based on the information below, write a professional design proposal that includes: an opening paragraph that reflects the designer's understanding of the client's vision and goals, a project scope section that lists the spaces and services included, a design process overview that walks through each phase from concept through installation, a timeline section with realistic phase durations, a fee structure section with placeholder amounts I will fill in, and a closing paragraph that reinforces the value of working together and invites them to move forward. Write in a warm, confident, elevated tone — this is a boutique studio, not a corporate firm. Avoid filler phrases like 'I am pleased to present' or 'It is with great pleasure.'"*

**[Paste in brief + notes from discovery call. Hit send.]**

**[CUT TO: Output generating. Scroll through it slowly.]**

Watch what happens to the opening paragraph. It's not generic. It's pulling directly from the client's own language — from the brief we built in Tutorial 02. The client who said they want "calm but not boring" will see exactly that reflected back to them.

**[Scroll to scope section.]**

The scope section is a clean list. I'll adjust the specifics, but the structure is already there. I'm not rebuilding a table from scratch.

**[Scroll to process section.]**

The process overview — this used to take me forty-five minutes to write from scratch because I wanted it to sound different every time without actually being different every time. Now I edit it in five.

**[Back to camera.]**

Here's the thing about AI-assisted proposals: clients don't know you used AI. And they wouldn't care if they did. What they experience is a beautifully written document that sounds like you understand them — because you do, and the AI helped you articulate it efficiently.

The places where you still bring something AI can't: your specific pricing, your instincts about what this particular client needs to hear, your relationship with the space. Everything else? First draft.

**[Pause.]**

One practice I've added: after I get the AI draft, I read it out loud. If anything sounds off — too formal, too stiff, not my voice — I fix it. It takes me about fifteen minutes to personalize a full proposal now. That's a hard fifteen minutes, where I'm making real decisions. Not the slow, exhausted hour I used to spend.

**[Direct to camera.]**

Your action step: take your most recent proposal — or a blank template you have — and run your last client brief through this prompt. Compare what you get with what you would have written. You don't have to use it. Just see what's possible.

Next up: we're talking about client communication. The emails. The check-ins. The "just wanted to update you" messages that are somehow never quick to write.
