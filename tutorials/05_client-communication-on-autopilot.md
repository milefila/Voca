# Tutorial 05: Client Communication on Autopilot
**Series:** Design Studio OS | Design Thread Studio
**Runtime:** ~4 minutes
**Format:** Talking head + screen share demo

---

## PRE-PRODUCTION NOTES

- **Tone:** Practical, a little playful. This is the tutorial with the most immediate relief. Lean into that.
- **Screen share:** Gmail or your email client, then Claude.ai side-by-side. Also show the AI-generated message dropped into a draft.
- **B-roll:** Phone notifications piling up, a "just checking in" thread, someone looking at their inbox with a tired expression.
- **Key pain point to validate:** It's not that client emails are hard to write — it's that there are so many of them, all slightly different, all requiring care.

---

## SCRIPT

**[OPEN ON: B-roll of inbox, many unread emails. Cut to camera.]**

There's a very specific kind of email fatigue that happens when you're managing multiple active projects.

It's not the big stuff — the scope conversations, the vendor negotiations, the difficult feedback. You know how to handle those. It's the in-between communications. The status update after a site visit. The check-in when procurement is just... waiting. The follow-up after you send a presentation and haven't heard back in four days.

These emails feel like they should take five minutes. They consistently take twenty. And by the end of a project-heavy week, you've sent forty of them.

**[Camera.]**

This is one of the fastest things to hand off to AI — not because the relationship doesn't matter, but because *most of these messages are structurally the same*. What changes is the project, the client name, and the specific update.

Let me show you the three templates I keep in my Design Studio OS that I use more than anything else.

**[TRANSITION TO SCREEN SHARE: Claude.ai or a saved document with prompts]**

**Template One: The Project Status Update.**

This is for when you need to tell a client where things stand — procurement is in process, lead times are tracking, here's what's next.

> *"Write a brief, warm project status update email for an interior design client. The designer's name is [Name], the project is [brief description], and the current status is: [paste 2-3 sentences of rough notes]. Include one thing that is on track, one realistic heads-up if there's anything pending or delayed, and a clear next step or what they can expect to hear from you next. Keep it under 150 words. Warm but professional — not overly formal."*

**[Show the output generating.]**

See how it takes rough notes — the kind you'd actually jot after a site visit — and shapes them into something that sounds considered and clear?

**[SCROLL TO SECOND TEMPLATE]**

**Template Two: The Follow-Up After Radio Silence.**

This one is delicate because you don't want to come across as pushy, but you genuinely need a response.

> *"Write a brief follow-up email for an interior design client who hasn't responded to a presentation sent [X] days ago. The designer sent [brief description of what was presented]. The tone should be warm, curious rather than impatient, and include a simple, low-friction call to action like 'Let me know if you'd like to jump on a quick call or if you have any questions.' Do not use phrases like 'just following up' or 'I wanted to circle back.' Under 100 words."*

**[Camera.]**

That "do not use" instruction matters. AI has a habit of defaulting to the phrases everyone's trying to escape. When you tell it explicitly what to avoid, it does.

**[TRANSITION TO THIRD TEMPLATE]**

**Template Three: The Difficult News Email.**

This is when something is delayed, over budget, or not going to plan and you need to communicate it in a way that maintains trust.

> *"Help me write an email to a design client sharing a difficult update. The situation is: [describe what happened — delay, vendor issue, etc.]. The tone should be direct, honest, and solution-focused — not apologetic to the point of seeming incompetent, but accountable and clear. Include: what happened, how it's being handled, and what the client can expect next. The designer maintains control and confidence throughout. Under 200 words."*

**[Back to camera.]**

This last one used to take me the longest to write because I'd rewrite it four times trying to find the right balance of apologetic and confident. Now I get a solid draft in thirty seconds and I spend my energy making sure the facts and tone are right for *that specific client* — not fighting with a blank page.

**[Pause.]**

One more thing. I recommend saving your five or six most-used prompts in a simple document — I keep mine in Notion — so they're always one click away. Don't retype them every time. Build your library once, then just drop in the specifics.

**[Direct to camera.]**

Your action step: identify the email you write most often in your business. Draft a prompt for it today. Send it through Claude and compare it to what you'd have written. You just automated a piece of your communication layer.

Last tutorial coming up — and it's one a lot of designers have been asking about. How to turn your finished projects into weeks of social content without sitting down to write posts from scratch.
