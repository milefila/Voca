# Tutorial 03: Concept Development in Half the Time
**Series:** Design Studio OS | Design Thread Studio
**Runtime:** ~5 minutes
**Format:** Talking head + screen share demo

---

## PRE-PRODUCTION NOTES

- **Tone:** Creative, energetic — this is about ideation. More expansive than tutorial 02.
- **Screen share:** Claude.ai for concept language generation, then a mood board platform (Milanote or Canva) to show the output applied.
- **B-roll:** Flipping through physical tear sheets, scrolling Pinterest endlessly — set up the old way.
- **Music:** Slightly more upbeat during the screen share demo.

---

## SCRIPT

**[OPEN ON: B-roll of scrolling Pinterest for a long time. Cut to camera.]**

Pinterest has a word I've started to think of as a productivity trap: "infinite scroll."

You open it looking for one thing — maybe a warm, layered living room with a hint of the Seventies — and ninety minutes later you've saved four hundred pins, none of which actually feel cohesive, and you've completely lost the thread of what made your client's brief compelling in the first place.

**[Camera.]**

The problem isn't inspiration. The problem is the path from a client brief to a clear design direction — a point of view you can actually communicate, sell, and build from.

AI doesn't do the taste-making. But it does something that is genuinely useful at this stage: it helps you name things.

Let me show you.

**[TRANSITION TO SCREEN SHARE: Claude.ai]**

I'm going to take the brief we generated in Tutorial 02 — the one about the client who wants a warm, functional living room for a family of four with a focus on natural materials and "calm but not boring" — and I'm going to use it to generate what I call a Concept Language Deck.

Here's the prompt:

**[SHOW ON SCREEN. Read aloud slowly.]**

> *"Based on the following design brief, generate a Concept Language Deck for this interior design project. The deck should include: a project concept title (evocative, not generic), a three-sentence concept statement that captures the mood, materiality, and emotional experience of the space, eight to ten aesthetic keywords that would guide sourcing decisions, a color story with specific descriptive names (not just 'beige' — think 'warm linen' or 'aged honey'), three to five reference eras or movements the design draws from, and a list of what this design is NOT (to help establish clear boundaries with the client). Avoid clichés and generic design language."*

**[Paste in the brief and hit send. Watch it generate.]**

**[CUT TO: Output appearing on screen. Read key parts aloud.]**

Look at this concept title: *"A House That Holds."*

That's the kind of language that makes a client feel seen. You didn't generate that title from a blank document at 10pm. You got there in forty seconds.

Now look at the "what this design is NOT" list. This is the part designers usually skip in presentations, and it's often what causes misalignment later. When you tell a client *"this is not cold, minimalist, or trend-driven"* — in writing, up front — you're doing preventative project management.

**[Back to camera.]**

Now here's where you come in.

**[Hold up a finger.]**

This output is a starting point. Your job is to read it and ask: does this feel true to what I heard from this client? Does the concept title actually match their personality? Is the color story heading somewhere I believe in?

Sometimes I take the output almost verbatim. Sometimes I edit two words and it suddenly becomes exactly right. Sometimes I throw out the concept title and keep the keyword list. It depends.

But I'm not starting from zero. I'm responding. And responding is faster and more generative than generating.

**[TRANSITION TO SCREEN SHARE: Milanote or Canva mood board]**

Once I have my Concept Language Deck, I open my mood board tool and I pin with intention. I'm not scrolling for whatever catches my eye. I'm searching specifically — looking for images that represent "aged honey" or "warm linen," that feel like "a house that holds." The keywords are a filter.

This takes me about twenty minutes now instead of two hours.

**[Back to camera.]**

Here's your action step: take an existing client brief — something you've already worked on — and run it through this prompt. See what the AI names. See what it gets right. See what it misses. You're not going to use the output for that client, but you're going to learn a lot about how to use it for the next one.

Next tutorial, we're talking about proposals. Which might be the biggest time sink in this business — and where AI saves designers the most hours per month.
