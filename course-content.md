### Lesson F1: The Shift — From Task Tool to Studio Partner
**Duration:** 8 minutes
**Outcome:** Understand why Claude is different from other AI tools — and why that difference changes how you work, not just how fast you work.

**Transcript:**

Most designers I talk to have tried an AI tool at some point. They used it to write an Instagram caption, maybe generated a few client email drafts, and then went back to doing it themselves. Not because AI didn't work — but because it felt like they were talking to a generic machine that didn't know anything about them, their clients, or what good design communication actually sounds like.

That's the gap this course is designed to close.

Claude is not a search engine you talk to. It's not an autocomplete tool. When you learn to work with it properly, it becomes something closer to a highly capable colleague — one who has read everything you've ever written about your studio, knows your client list, understands your aesthetic, and never forgets what you told it last Tuesday.

The shift I'm asking you to make is this: stop thinking about AI as a shortcut for individual tasks, and start thinking about it as infrastructure. You're not using Claude to write one email. You're building a system where your communication, your operations, and your creative process all have an intelligent, always-on partner running alongside them.

That's what Design Studio OS is. Not a collection of prompts — a studio-wide operating system built on Claude.

Here's what makes this possible: Claude can hold context. You can give it a detailed brief about your studio, your clients, your communication style, and your non-negotiables — and then work from that foundation across every conversation. The designers who get transformative results from Claude aren't prompting harder. They're prompting smarter, with systems behind each conversation.

By the end of this course, you'll have six of those systems built and running.

### Lesson F2: Setting Up Your Claude Workspace
**Duration:** 12 minutes
**Outcome:** Have Claude Pro set up and configured with your first Project, ready to use by Lesson F3.

**Transcript:**

Before we build anything, we need the workspace. This lesson walks through setup — Claude Pro, Projects, and how to organize your workspace so it's studio-ready from day one.

First: Claude Pro. The free tier of Claude will let you experiment, but for a working studio, you need Pro. The reason is Projects — Claude's built-in feature for maintaining persistent context across conversations. Without Pro, every conversation starts cold. With Projects, Claude can hold your studio brief, your voice document, and your current client list across every session. That persistence is what makes everything else in this course work.

Setting up Claude Pro:
1. Go to claude.ai and sign up or log into your account
2. Upgrade to Claude Pro — as of this recording, it's $20/month
3. Navigate to "Projects" in the left sidebar
4. Create a new Project — name it something like "Design Studio OS" or your studio name

Projects work like dedicated workspaces. You can upload reference documents, set a persistent system prompt (what Anthropic calls "Project Instructions"), and everything Claude learns in that project stays in that project. You can have multiple Projects — one for client communications, one for operations, one for creative work. We'll build all three during this course.

For now, create one Project and leave it blank. We'll add the first real context in Module 1 when we build your Thread Framework.

The other thing worth knowing: Claude's context window. In any given conversation, Claude can hold roughly 150,000 words of context — that's a short novel. For a working session, this means you can paste in a full project brief, several email threads, and a specification document all at once. Don't be afraid to give Claude a lot of information. More context almost always produces better output.

- [ ] Sign up for Claude Pro (claude.ai)
- [ ] Create your first Project — name it "[Studio Name] OS"
- [ ] Explore the Projects panel and Project Instructions field
- [ ] Note the difference between Projects and regular conversations

### Lesson F3: How to Talk to Claude — Prompting for Practitioners
**Duration:** 14 minutes
**Outcome:** Write your first effective prompt and understand the three elements that determine output quality.

**Transcript:**

There's a reason some designers get magical results from Claude and others get mediocre ones — and it's not the tool. It's the conversation.

Prompting isn't about finding the magic words. It's about giving Claude the three things it needs to produce excellent output: role, context, and constraints.

**Role** tells Claude who it's being asked to be. "Help me write an email" is weak. "You are a boutique interior design studio director writing to a high-net-worth client who asked for a second revision after we thought the concept was approved" is a role. The more specific the role, the more calibrated the response.

**Context** gives Claude the facts of the situation. This is where most designers under-invest. Don't just ask Claude to write your proposal intro — paste in your notes from the discovery call, the client's stated aesthetic preferences, your fee structure, and the scope of work. More input equals better output. Always.

**Constraints** tell Claude what not to do. These are as important as what you ask for. "Keep it under 150 words." "Don't use the word 'bespoke.'" "Match the tone of the email I paste below." Constraints prevent Claude from defaulting to generic, which is its natural gravitational pull without guidance.

A prompt that uses all three looks like this:

> [Role] You are a boutique interior design studio director. [Context] A client named Sarah Harrington hired us for a full living room and primary bedroom redesign, $85k budget. She's seen the initial concept, loved the overall direction, but is asking to swap out the primary sofa — she found one she likes on 1stDibs and wants our opinion. Our honest view is that the sofa doesn't work with the palette but we want to handle this diplomatically. [Constraints] Write a 3-paragraph email response. Be warm but direct. Don't capitulate on the design direction but leave her feeling heard. End with a clear next step.

That prompt takes 60 seconds to write and produces a first draft you'd be proud to send.

We'll practice this pattern throughout the course. For now, try it with the warmup exercise in your checklist.

**Checklist:**
- [ ] Write your first Role + Context + Constraints prompt about a real studio scenario
- [ ] Send it to Claude and review the output
- [ ] Revise the prompt once based on what was missing from the first response
- [ ] Save the refined prompt in your Claude Project

### Lesson F4: Your Studio Audit — What We're Building and Why
**Duration:** 11 minutes
**Outcome:** Complete a studio audit that identifies your five biggest operational pain points, which we'll address directly across Modules 1–5.

**Transcript:**

Before we build, we audit. This lesson gives you a structured way to look at your studio as a system — and identify where Claude will have the highest impact fastest.

The Studio Audit has five sections: Communication, Client Experience, Operations, Creative Process, and Finance. In each section, you're looking for one thing: where are you spending time that doesn't require your expertise?

**Communication** — Count how many emails you send per week that follow a similar pattern. Inquiry responses, status updates, revision requests, vendor coordination. Every repeatable communication is a candidate for systemization.

**Client Experience** — Map the journey from first contact to final invoice. Where do projects get stuck? Where do clients get anxious? Where do you feel like you're explaining the same thing to every client?

**Operations** — What's in your head that isn't written down? If you had to onboard a junior designer tomorrow, what would fall apart?

**Creative Process** — Where do you spend time on the scaffolding of creative work rather than the creative work itself? Research, sourcing rationale, presentation formatting, proposal writing.

**Finance** — How much time per month do you spend on budget tracking, markup calculations, and invoice management? Is it clear to you in any given week how your projects are tracking financially?

Your answers to these questions become the roadmap for this course. Each module addresses one of these five areas — not in theory, but by building actual systems you'll use immediately.

**Checklist:**
- [ ] Complete the Studio Audit worksheet (attached to this lesson)
- [ ] Rank your five biggest pain points by time cost
- [ ] Write a one-sentence description of your "ideal state" for each area
- [ ] Save your audit in your Claude Project as a reference document

**Deliverable:** Completed Studio Audit worksheet — your roadmap for the next five modules.

### Lesson 1.1: Anatomy of a Thread — Voice, Context, Constraints
**Duration:** 10 minutes
**Outcome:** Understand the structure of a Thread and why it's different from a single prompt.

**Transcript:**

A Thread is a structured prompt document — not a one-liner, but a brief you give Claude before any conversation in a given category. Think of it like a creative brief you'd give a copywriter, except Claude reads it in seconds and applies it immediately.

A Thread has three layers:

**Layer 1 — Identity.** Who are you in this context? Your studio name, your positioning, your aesthetic sensibility, and the kind of work you do. This layer tells Claude the world it's working in.

**Layer 2 — Audience.** Who are you talking to? Client Threads define the specific person or type — their background, their communication preferences, how formal or warm the register should be, what they're anxious about, what they respond to.

**Layer 3 — Rules.** What are the non-negotiables? Your words you never use. The topics you don't address over email. The boundaries you hold on pricing conversations. The way you handle conflict. Rules are where your Thread becomes truly yours.

When all three layers are in place, you give Claude a Thread at the start of a conversation, paste in your situation, and the output lands in the right voice, for the right person, within your professional standards.

We'll build your first Thread in Lesson 1.4. This lesson is about understanding the architecture so the build makes sense.

**Checklist:**

- [ ] For each, note: who are you talking to? What outcome do you need?
- [ ] Draft a rough version of your "Identity Layer" — who you are as a studio

### Lesson 1.2: Writing Your Studio Voice Document
**Duration:** 14 minutes
**Outcome:** A completed Studio Voice Document saved in your Claude Project.

**Transcript:**

The Studio Voice Document is the foundation of everything you'll build in this course. It's a 300–500 word brief that tells Claude who you are — not your resume, but your operating standards.

It covers five things:

**Tone markers.** Words that describe how your communication sounds. Refined but not precious. Direct but never cold. Decisive but open to dialogue. These aren't adjectives for a mood board — they're constraints for every output Claude writes in your name.

**Non-vocabulary.** Words you never use. "Bespoke." "Curated." "Journey." "Elevate." Whatever makes you wince. List them explicitly. Claude will avoid them without being asked again.

**Communication principles.** How you handle difficult conversations. Whether you prefer to address conflict in writing or call first. How you acknowledge client concerns before responding. Your standard for response turnaround.

**Studio positioning.** What you do and who it's for. Your price point, your client type, your geographic focus if it matters. Enough context that Claude understands the professional world you operate in.

**Voice sample.** Paste in one email or proposal excerpt you're proud of. This is the best single tool for calibrating Claude to your actual voice — more than any description.

When you're done, save this document in your Claude Project under Project Instructions. Every conversation in that Project will now have your voice as default context.

### Lesson 1.3: Building Your Client Persona Library
**Duration:** 12 minutes
**Outcome:** 3–5 Client Persona documents ready to use as Thread layers.

**Transcript:**

Not every client conversation is the same. A recently retired couple renovating their home for the first time needs a different communication style than a developer who's on their fifth project with you. A creative-director client who wants to be involved in every decision needs a different Thread than the executive who hired you specifically to make decisions without her.

Your Client Persona Library is a set of 3–5 brief profiles — one for each archetypal client you work with. Each profile covers:

- **Background:** Who are they professionally and personally?
- **Communication style:** Formal or casual? Detailed or high-level? Fast-paced or deliberate?
- **Primary concern:** Budget certainty? Aesthetic alignment? Timeline? Control?
- **Relationship dynamic:** Are they deferential to your expertise or do they want to be a co-designer?
- **Common friction points:** What tends to go sideways with this type?

You'll use these profiles in the Audience Layer of your Threads. When a new project starts, you identify which persona fits this client (or blend two), drop it into your Thread, and Claude's output shifts accordingly.

This is one of the highest-leverage investments you'll make in the course. A well-built Persona Library means you never have to explain your client to Claude twice.

**Checklist:**
- [ ] Identify your 3–5 most common client archetypes
- [ ] Complete a Persona profile for each using the template (attached)
- [ ] Save each Persona as a separate document in your Claude Project
- [ ] Test one Persona: ask Claude to draft a difficult message for that client type

### Lesson 1.4: Thread Templates for Every Client Scenario
**Duration:** 14 minutes
**Outcome:** 5 complete, ready-to-use Thread Templates covering your most common communication scenarios.

**Transcript:**

Now we build. In this lesson, you'll create five Threads — each one a complete, ready-to-deploy prompt document for a recurring communication scenario.

The five scenarios every studio needs covered:

1. **The Inquiry Response Thread** — First contact from a prospective client. Goal: warm, professional, qualifying without being interrogative. Sets the tone for everything that follows.

2. **The Scope Clarification Thread** — When you need to clarify what's in and out of scope without it feeling like a disagreement. Goal: firm but collaborative.

3. **The Revision Limit Thread** — When a client has used their included revisions and wants more. Goal: set the boundary clearly without damaging the relationship.

4. **The Delay Notification Thread** — When a delivery is going to be late — yours or a vendor's. Goal: proactive, owning the situation, providing a clear path forward.

5. **The Difficult Feedback Thread** — When a client is unhappy and you need to respond to criticism of your work. Goal: acknowledge without capitulating, protect the relationship while holding your professional ground.

For each Thread, you'll combine your Studio Voice Document, the relevant Client Persona, and a scenario-specific Rule layer. The output is a document you can paste into any Claude conversation and immediately get drafts that are on-brief.

**Checklist:**
- [ ] Build all 5 Thread Templates using the provided structure
- [ ] Test each Thread with a real or hypothetical scenario
- [ ] Refine based on Claude's first response
- [ ] Save all 5 Threads in your Claude Project

### Lesson 1.5: Capstone — Your First Five Live Threads
**Duration:** 10 minutes
**Outcome:** 5 Threads deployed in real studio scenarios and refined based on actual results.

**Transcript:**

Your capstone for Module 1 is simple: use your Threads. Take the five you built in Lesson 1.4 and deploy them in real or near-real scenarios this week. If you don't have all five situations live right now, that's fine — simulate the ones you don't have. Write the scenario, drop in the Thread, see how Claude responds.

After each deployment, do a 60-second debrief:
- Did the output sound like you?
- Did Claude miss anything important about the scenario?
- Is there a constraint you should add to the Thread?

Refine once, and save the updated version. Your Threads will compound in quality every time you use them.

**Deliverable:** 5 completed, tested, and refined Thread Templates saved in your Claude Project — your studio communication foundation.
